## The Corner

He was not running. He was walking fast enough that the distinction was mostly private, a slice of peanut-butter toast held between his teeth.

Someone came around the blind corner at the same moment he did. He moved left. She moved the same way. They both corrected and collided anyway.

The toast slipped from his teeth. He caught it by the crust.

“Sorry.”

“I’m fine.” Her eyes went to the toast in his hand. “I thought this only happened in shoujo manga.”

He looked at the toast.

“It has peanut butter,” he said.

She looked back at him. “I don’t think that changes the genre.”

She stepped past him and continued along the street.

He waited a few seconds before resuming the route he had already intended to take.

At the next turning, she went left. So did he.

At the entrance to a building, she stopped to check something on her phone. Several paces behind her, he checked the address on his. She noticed him. Neither spoke.

She went inside.

At the end of the corridor, an A4 notice beside a half-open door marked the room for the talk. She read it and entered. He checked it after her, although he already knew what it said.

Only a few of the chairs were occupied. She chose a seat in the front half of the room. He sat several rows behind her and across the aisle.

## The Talk

The lights dimmed. The speaker began.

On the screen, a page of text gave way to a title.

> PATCHWORK GIRL  
> SHELLEY JACKSON / AFTER MARY SHELLEY

*Frankenstein*. Still his favourite British science-fiction novel. The prose could be absurdly mannered. That was part of why he liked it.

The screen filled and cleared several times. A few people typed. Someone near the door coughed.

The next title he noticed was:

> CALVINO  
> THE BURNING OF THE ABOMINABLE HOUSE

*Calvino. Who?*

More slides passed.

> STORY SIFTING  
> SELECT THE UNEXPECTED

This time his attention left the screen.

The woman from the corner sat several rows ahead, her face turned towards the speaker.

The *bishoujo* in his head had never looked like her. Yet by his own private reading of each of the word’s three characters, she was one. Annoyingly, he could not argue himself out of it.

When he looked back, the slide had changed.

The talk continued. When the lights came up, the sparse audience gathered itself and moved towards the door. She left with the first few people.

By the time he reached the corridor, she was gone.

## Three Bad Mornings

For three weeks running, the one morning he took the Tube was the morning something went wrong.

An announcement broke apart in the noise above the platform. People looked up, then at one another, and began to move before he had caught more than an apology.

Across the crowd, he saw the woman from the corner. Her head turned. Before he could tell whether she had seen him, people crossed between them. When the gap opened, she was gone.

*Cut.*

Another announcement. Another crowd changing its mind.

This time she saw him first.

“You again.”

He opened his mouth. The crowd changed direction once more and took her one way and him another.

*Cut.*

By the third week, he had begun moving before he made out the words. Part of the line was suspended. Everyone was being sent up to the street and towards replacement buses that had not yet arrived.

He found her in the crowd. She saw him too.

They came out of the station a few steps apart. On the pavement, the crowd spread towards the replacement queue. When it thinned, they were still walking in the same direction.

## The Walk

The queue was already folding back along the pavement.

“I’m going to walk to the Strand,” he said.

“All the way?”

“I usually do.”

She looked back at the queue. “I’m going there too. I think I’ll walk.”

They left the queue behind.

*The station-side background gives way to a road: buses and cars form a steady lateral blur, while the pavement and the space around the characters remain clear.*

“I never got your name,” he said.

“Neither did I.”

“He Xuyuan.”

“What should I call you?”

“Xuyuan.”

“Xuyuan,” she repeated carefully. “I’m Lulu.”

“Lulu,” he repeated.

Lulu looked towards the next crossing. The signal changed against them before they reached it.

“Do you have a class?” he asked as they waited.

“A lecture. You?”

“A seminar.”

When they started again, she glanced at him.

“You walk this every day?”

“Most days. It’s cheaper.”

“Why did you take the Tube today?”

“I overslept.”

“All three times?”

He looked at her.

“Yes.”

They walked for a while before he spoke again.

“What did you think of the talk?”

“I liked it. I was actually looking for the room next door.”

“But you stayed.”

“It was interesting.” She looked at him. “What did you think?”

“I liked it.”

“Was it for your course?”

“Not officially.”

“What do you study?”

“Digital culture. English literature before that.”

“That’s quite a change.”

“It didn’t feel like one.”

“What about you?”

“War Studies.”

At the next crossing, they stopped with everyone else. Traffic passed. When the road cleared, they crossed and walked most of the next block without speaking.

At the edge of the campus, Lulu slowed.

“My lecture’s this way.”

“Right.”

“See you.”

“See you.”

She turned towards one entrance. He continued towards another.

## The Following Week

The following week, nothing went wrong.

No announcement rearranged the platform. The train arrived, the doors opened, and nobody was sent back towards the stairs.

He saw Lulu farther down the carriage. She saw him and came over.

“You overslept again.”

“Yes.”

The doors closed without incident.

“Nothing’s gone wrong,” he said.

“I know.”

## The Pattern

*Later that day. A café off Kingsway.*

His friend Kai was halfway through lunch when Xuyuan began.

“Most people’s interests are activities. Films. Games. Sport. Mine is *bishoujo*. Each of its three characters is a separate dimension. How I read all three is entirely up to me.”

His friend waited.

“That part is new.”

“It has always been implicit. But if all three readings are mine, then I should decide what I mean and afterwards decide whether someone fits. This time I noticed the second decision first. At the talk I looked at a woman and realised I had already put her in the category before I could have said why. I had to work backwards from there.”

“It was still your judgement.”

“I know. The result was not unusual. The order was.”

His friend took another bite. “Who was she?”

“Her name is Lulu.”

“VTuber?”

“War Studies.”

His friend nodded as if this had answered the question.

“She was the woman I walked into outside that talk. She had meant to go to the room next door, but found ours interesting and stayed. Then, for three Thursdays in a row, I overslept, took the Tube and found a different part of the system failing each time. The first week I saw her across the crowd and lost sight of her. The second week she saw me first and spoke, but the crowd separated us. The third week the suspended line sent us both to the street, so we walked to campus together.”

He paused. His friend did not fill the pause.

“This morning nothing failed. She was farther down the carriage. She saw me and came over anyway. By then it felt as though fate had been staging the same meeting again and again, each time letting it run a little longer.”

“Fate has an unusually detailed knowledge of service disruptions.”

“There is no fate involved. Network failures and emergent crowd behaviour can explain how we met on each of the first three mornings separately. They do not bind four Thursdays into a single causal system. But ‘not arranged by fate’ does not mean ‘formless’. The causes changed. She was the refrain. Each time, one less external thing had to happen for the interaction to continue. Being certain it was coincidence would not turn what happened into noise. Probability is not an instruction to be unimpressed.”

His friend set down his fork. “You have rehearsed that.”

“I thought of it while I was speaking.”

“That is not better. You selected the sequence after it happened. London gives millions of people broken journeys and repeated strangers. Of course your selected set has a refrain. You selected it by the person who recurred.”

“That means I cannot use the pattern as evidence that someone intended it. It does not follow that I should discard it.”

“Noise is relative to a question. And you are not counting service failures. You are counting appearances of Lulu.”

“Yes.”

“And this morning Lulu walked down a carriage to speak to you when nothing was wrong. You’re not usually one of those ‘a woman spoke to me, so she must be interested’ people.”

“I did not say she was interested. I said the structure changed. Until today, the system did the moving. It altered the route, moved the crowd and put us on the pavement. Today the system worked, and she moved.”

“She walked down a carriage.”

“That is what I said.”

“You needed the whole transport network to say that.”

His friend returned to his lunch.

“So she’s cute.”

“That is an aggressive compression.”

“Is it wrong?”

Xuyuan did not answer at once.

“It is incomplete.”

## Mallowbell Maid Café

*Saturday afternoon. A side street north of Holborn.*

The painted sign above the door read:

> MALLOWBELL MAID CAFÉ

“You said it was nearby,” Xuyuan said.

“It is.”

“You omitted several relevant properties.”

Kai looked up at the sign. “They were on the sign.”

He opened the door. A bell rang above them.

“Welcome home, Masters.”

Xuyuan had known what sort of place it was. Knowledge had not prepared him to be welcomed home as a master before he had taken off his coat.

The maid at the door asked whether it was their first visit.

“For both of us,” Kai said.

She explained the table time and minimum order in the clear voice of someone who had explained them many times. They could photograph themselves and their food, but not the maids without permission. Instant photographs with a maid were a separate item on the menu.

Kai answered the practical questions. Xuyuan followed them to a four-person table beneath a line of paper blossoms and sat opposite him.

Kai opened the menu and stopped at the desserts.

“They have the matcha parfait.”

“You knew that.”

“Yes.”

“Before we came.”

“Yes.”

Kai kept one finger on the page and showed no further uncertainty about his order.

The bell above the door rang again.

“Welcome home, Princesses.”

Xuyuan looked up.

Lulu stood just inside the door with another woman. She saw him at almost the same moment.

“You again.”

“Hello.”

Kai looked at Xuyuan once and then at the menu.

The café was nearly full. After speaking to Lulu and her friend, the maid came to their table.

“We’re quite busy at the moment. Would both parties be comfortable sharing the large table?”

“Of course,” Kai said.

“That’s fine,” the other woman said.

Before Xuyuan had moved anything, Kai brought his glass and menu around to the empty place beside him. Lulu’s friend sat opposite Kai. Lulu took the remaining chair, opposite Xuyuan.

“You two know each other?” her friend asked.

“We keep meeting,” Lulu said.

“I’m Anna, by the way.”

“Kai Zhou. Kai is fine.”

Lulu looked across the table. “And this is Xuyuan.”

He looked at her.

“You told me,” she said.

Anna glanced between the two men. “Are you both at King’s?”

“No, LSE,” Kai said.

“Xuyuan is at King’s. Digital Culture,” Lulu said.

He looked at her again.

“You also told me that.”

“What do you do at LSE?” Anna asked Kai.

“Philosophy of the social sciences.”

“Master’s?”

“PhD.”

“That sounds broad.”

“The thesis isn’t.”

“Lulu and I both study War Studies,” Anna said. “We met through the society.”

She picked up her menu and looked around the café.

“This was my idea.”

“Mine too,” Kai said.

“So they can blame us.”

“That seems fair.”

When the maid returned, Anna gave her order first. Lulu chose the curry omelette rice. Xuyuan chose the classic omelette rice. Kai ordered the matcha parfait without reopening the menu.

Anna turned to the illustrated instructions beside the food photographs.

“It says we have to cast a spell.”

“The version everyone recognises—the omelette rice and *moe moe kyun*—comes from *K-ON!*,” Xuyuan said.

“No,” Lulu said. “Maid cafés in Akihabara were already using the line. *K-ON!* made it famous.”

Anna looked at her. “I thought this was your first time.”

“It is. I still know things.”

“So you know the spell, not the procedure,” Xuyuan said.

“Do you know the procedure?”

“I arrived five minutes before you.”

Kai, whose experience was also five minutes long, left them to it.

The food arrived in stages. The maid set Xuyuan’s omelette rice in front of him with an unopened bottle of ketchup.

“What would you like me to draw?”

He had not prepared an artistic preference.

“Can it really be anything?” Anna asked.

“Anything simple,” the maid said.

“A cat, please,” Xuyuan said.

She drew a round face, pointed ears and whiskers across the yellow omelette. The result was recognisably a cat and happier than any cat Xuyuan had met.

The curry omelette rice went in front of Lulu. Kai’s parfait arrived in a tall glass, green and white beneath a crown of cream.

Before anyone picked up a spoon, the maid brought her hands together.

“I need all our masters and princesses to help me cast the magic that makes everything delicious. Hands like this. Ready?”

Xuyuan had been trying to find a socially neutral amount of participation. Lulu had already made the heart. Anna followed. Kai did the same with the untroubled precision of someone completing the next step in a procedure.

Xuyuan raised his hands.

“Become delicious—*moe, moe, kyun!*”

Four hearts moved towards the table. The maid thanked them in her ordinary voice and went to the next group.

Anna looked at Kai’s parfait. “Worth it?”

Kai took a spoonful.

“Yes.”

For a while, the four of them ate.

“You chose the curry one,” Xuyuan said to Lulu.

“Yes.”

“Do you prefer curry or rice?”

The question became stupid as soon as he heard it outside his head. Curry was not an alternative to rice. It was currently resting on it.

“That was a stupid question,” he said.

“A little. But I know what you meant. I prefer curry.”

“I prefer rice.”

Lulu looked at him. “Was that why you asked?”

“Not entirely. I wanted to know which part made you choose it. Rice is usually treated as whatever happens underneath the answer. This rice is too wet. It hasn’t rested properly, and the grains have stopped being individual grains.”

Lulu looked down at the rice beneath her curry.

“Mine tastes fine.”

“It is the same rice.”

“Then yours tastes fine too.”

“I make better rice.”

“I’m sure you do.”

He used brown rice and long-grain rice together. The ratio depended on what had to go with it. The water mattered, and so did leaving the lid closed long enough after the heat went off. If Lulu tried it, she would understand that rice was not merely the substance beneath something more interesting.

The proposition arrived with a kitchen, an evening and Lulu seated at a table while he lifted the lid. It omitted the smaller problem that they had no arrangement to meet again.

“Your cat is getting cold,” Lulu said.

Xuyuan looked down. One ketchup whisker had begun to run.

“Right.”

The plates gradually emptied. Conversation loosened, then settled. Around them, greetings rose from the door and spoons struck glass.

Anna looked above the counter. “Are those meant to be cherry blossoms?”

“I think so,” Lulu said.

She turned back to Xuyuan. “Are the real ones out yet?”

“Some of them should be. I haven’t checked.”

“I haven’t seen them properly since I came here. I’d like to, before they’re gone.”

“There are several places.”

“If you find somewhere good, send it to me.”

“I don’t have your number.”

“Oh.”

Lulu took out her phone. “Do you use WhatsApp?”

“Yes.”

They exchanged numbers across the table. Her name appeared on his screen: Lulu.

Neither friend commented.

When they had paid, the maid accompanied them to the door.

“Safe travels, Masters and Princesses. Come home again soon.”

Outside, the four of them stopped at the next corner.

“We’re going this way,” Anna said.

Lulu lifted one hand. “Send me what you find.”

“I will.”

She and Anna crossed the road. Kai began walking in the other direction. Xuyuan went with him.
