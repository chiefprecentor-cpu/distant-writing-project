# Shape VN Rhythm and Montage

Read rhythm across the channels that the work actually uses. Word count and dialogue count are clues, not substitutes for experienced pace.

For the current distant-writing project, read the shared [aesthetic profile](../interpret-co-writing-signals/references/aesthetic-profile.md).

If the author supplies a whole sequence, inspect it in full and include neighbouring scenes when available. Locate rhythmic changes yourself. An isolated montage paragraph cannot reveal whether the reader had time to enter the prior scene or land after the cut.

Consider, as relevant:

- changes in relationship or information per beat;
- number of speaker switches versus the length and internal movement of each turn;
- action, silence, ambient sound, image change, and unshown duration;
- repetition, variation, acceleration, interruption, and the moment a short pattern opens into sustained time;
- whether omitted conversation feels like elegant compression, missing social substance, or intentional montage;
- whether a cut creates association, estrangement, pressure, or merely skips an awkward transition;
- how quickly the reader must rebuild character, place, and emotional orientation after successive cuts.

The current preference leans towards sparse montage because cuts can fragment reading. The project's unusual narration, repeated structures, and fallible observer concealed behind an apparently third-person, near-omniscient surface can also make montage unusually productive. Treat both as starting considerations. Deliberate fragmentation may itself be the intended effect; no count, ratio, or mandatory landing length decides the question.

Use a beat map or density sketch when it clarifies the relationship. Give the high-leverage adjustments the material supports and explain what each would change. When authorised to revise, prefer a focused change whose scale matches the chosen effect.
